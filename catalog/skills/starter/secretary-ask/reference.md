# Secretary ask: examples and templates

## A child ask, good

> The boss is deciding on Thursday whether to move the checkout launch from 14 to 28
> November. Give the boss your honest completion range for the checkout project and the
> reason for each end of the range. I know the payment provider sandbox passed on 2
> November; I do not know the state of the tax rules. Name the two risks you would watch
> most, with an owner and what would raise the likelihood. Answer by 17:00 today.

## A child ask, weak

> Send me the status of the checkout project.

It gives no context, asks for everything, and says nothing about when or in what form.

## The answer format to request

Put this in every child, so the answers can be read the same way:

1. **Answer:** the direct answer to the question, in one or two sentences.
2. **Confidence and why:** high, medium or low, and the reason.
3. **Evidence:** links to the issues or comments that support each claim, with the date
   you read them.
4. **Risks:** each with its cause, its likelihood, its impact, and its owner.
5. **Not checked:** what you could not verify, and why.
6. **Blockers:** what stops the answer from being firmer, and who can clear it.

A leader may answer with the same structure in a comment. The analysis reads each
section against its evidence.

## Choosing addressees

Question: "Is the Mua Ngon project on track for its September milestone?"

- The project belongs to the squad whose leader owns it. That is the narrow addressee.
- The map shows a dependency on the Platform squad. The question touches it, so ask it too.
- A third squad's open work does not touch the project. Leave it out and say so in the
  reply: "I did not ask the Research squad; it has no work on this project."
